Day 12 — How much do the plants differ?

Lecture slides · Quiz · Weekly revision

Today’s question

“Control seedlings grew 15.3 mm on average” does not tell a reader whether every plant grew about 15 mm or whether some grew 8 mm and others 19 mm. Today you describe both the centre of a set of measurements and their spread, first with three small numbers you can check by hand and then with the seedlings.

Use the complete file data/seedlings.csv today, not yesterday’s repair exercise; the study is described in the data dictionary.

Save today: day12-variation.R in the project root.

Mean and median

The mean adds all values and divides by how many there are. The median is the middle value after sorting, or the average of the two middle values when there is an even number. Use both to find out where three growth values are centred:

growth_mm <- c(12, 14, 16)
mean(growth_mm)
median(growth_mm)

Both give 14 mm. Now one value is much larger than the others:

uneven_mm <- c(10, 10, 10, 100)
mean(uneven_mm)
median(uneven_mm)

The mean is 32.5 mm and the median 10 mm. The large value pulls the mean strongly but hardly moves the median. Neither number is wrong; if the large value is a real measurement, it stays in the data.

How far is each value from the mean?

The deviation of a value is its distance from the mean: value minus mean. For 12, 14 and 16 mm the deviations are −2, 0 and +2 mm. They always add up to zero, because the mean sits in the middle, so their plain average cannot measure spread. Squaring each deviation removes the sign: 4, 0 and 4.

Variance and standard deviation

The sample variance adds the squared deviations and divides by one less than the number of values. The standard deviation (SD) is the square root of the variance. Use R to find out both for the three growth values, once by the formula and once with the built-in functions:

deviations_mm <- growth_mm - mean(growth_mm)
sum(deviations_mm^2) / (length(growth_mm) - 1)
var(growth_mm)
sd(growth_mm)

The squared deviations add up to 8, and 8 / 2 gives a variance of 4 mm². The SD is 2 mm. Because the variance squares millimetres, its unit is mm², which is hard to picture; the SD is back in millimetres and can be read next to the mean. Dividing by n − 1 instead of n is the usual choice when the values are a sample, and var() and sd() do the same.

Now compare two toy groups with the same mean:

close_mm <- c(13, 14, 15)
spread_mm <- c(8, 14, 20)
mean(close_mm)
mean(spread_mm)
sd(close_mm)
sd(spread_mm)

Both means are 14 mm, but the SDs are 1 mm and 6 mm: the plants in the second group differ much more from each other.

How much do the control plants vary?

Use the same functions on the control plants to find out their centre and spread:

library(readr)
library(dplyr)
seedlings <- read_csv("data/seedlings.csv", show_col_types = FALSE) |>
  mutate(growth_mm = final_mm - initial_mm)
control <- filter(seedlings, treatment == "control")
mean(control$growth_mm)
median(control$growth_mm)
var(control$growth_mm)
sd(control$growth_mm)

The 12 control plants grew a mean of 15.28 mm (median 15.65 mm) with an SD of 3.19 mm and a variance of 10.18 mm². None of them grew exactly 15.28 mm: their growth ranges from 8.0 to 18.7 mm. In a report, round to what the ruler can support, for example “mean 15.3 mm, SD 3.2 mm, n = 12”.

Centre and spread in the seedlings

  1. Write the three-row hand calculation for 12, 14 and 16 mm in a comment in day12-variation.R: each value, its deviation, its squared deviation, their sum, and the divisor n − 1. Check that it gives the same 4 mm² as var().
  2. Use mean(), median(), var() and sd() on the treated plants to find out whether they differ from the controls in centre, in spread, or in both. You should get 21.49 mm, 20.85 mm, 23.67 mm² and 4.87 mm: the treated plants grew more and varied more.
  3. Use the same four functions on c(12, 14, 26) to find out what one unusually tall plant does to each summary. Predict first. The mean rises from 14 to 17.33 mm, the median stays 14 mm, and the SD jumps from 2 to 7.57 mm.
  4. Use mean(close_mm + 10) and sd(close_mm + 10) to find out what changes if every plant had grown 10 mm more. The mean becomes 24 mm and the SD stays 1 mm, because the plants still differ from each other by the same amounts.
  5. Divide close_mm by 10 to convert to centimetres and find out the new mean, variance and SD. You get 1.4 cm, 0.01 cm² and 0.1 cm: the mean and SD are divided by 10, the variance by 100. The plants are not less variable; only the unit changed. A check like this is useful when your code converts units.
Hint: use the units to check yourself

Adding a constant moves the centre but leaves every deviation unchanged. Dividing lengths by ten divides the mean and SD by ten, and the variance by one hundred because its unit is squared. ?var and ?sd describe the functions and their na.rm argument for missing values.

Another lab: percentage yield

Your teacher may choose this context instead of task 2 above. In an organic synthesis, each run is one attempt to make a product. In data/organic-recovery.csv, theoretical_mass_g is the most product a run could give from its starting materials, and isolated_mass_g is the dry product actually collected, both in grams. Runs started from different amounts, so you compare the percentage yield, 100 × isolated / theoretical, rather than grams.

Use mean(), var() and sd() to find out how much the yield of the standard protocol varies between runs:

organic <- read_csv("data/organic-recovery.csv", show_col_types = FALSE) |>
  mutate(yield_percent = 100 * isolated_mass_g / theoretical_mass_g)
standard_runs <- filter(organic, protocol == "standard")
mean(standard_runs$yield_percent)
var(standard_runs$yield_percent)
sd(standard_runs$yield_percent)

Run Y01 gives 100 × 0.62 / 1.00 = 62%. The six standard runs have a mean yield of 68.67%, a variance of 18.67 squared percentage points and an SD of 4.32 percentage points. A change from 62% to 70% is 8 percentage points, not an 8% increase. Repeat the summaries for protocol == "modified": mean 77.00%, SD 3.74 percentage points.

A second class’s data

The second class’s file, data/seedlings-transfer.csv, has 18 plants, nine per group. Do their treated plants also vary more than their controls? Start day12-transfer.R with its own library() lines, import and growth calculation.

  1. Find T01 and check its growth by hand (15.0 mm), so you know the subtraction is the right way round.
  2. Use group_by(treatment) and summarise() with n(), mean(), median(), var() and sd() to find out the centre and spread of each group, and save the table as output/day12-transfer-summaries.csv. Control: n 9, mean 13.66 mm, median 12.9 mm, SD 2.53 mm. Treated: n 9, mean 17.87 mm, median 17.1 mm, SD 1.94 mm. Here the treated plants grew more but varied less, the opposite of the first study, so a larger mean does not come with a larger SD.
  3. Add a column growth_cm = growth_mm / 10 and summarise it again to find out which numbers change with the unit. Means and SDs are divided by 10 and variances by 100, and the treated group still has the larger mean and the smaller SD.

Check your understanding

Which spread is larger in the main study, and what does it describe?

The treated SD (4.87 mm) is larger than the control SD (3.19 mm). It describes how much individual treated plants differ from each other. How precisely we know a group’s mean is a different question, which comes on day 14.

Does a larger SD mean the measurements were careless?

Not necessarily. Plants really differ, and measurement noise adds to that. The SD shows how much the values vary, not why they vary.
