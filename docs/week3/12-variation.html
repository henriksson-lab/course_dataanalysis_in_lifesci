Day 12 — An average does not describe every plant

Lecture slides · Weekly self-check

Today’s question

Two groups can have the same average growth yet look very different. How can we say where measurements are centred and how much individual plants vary? Today we use small numbers first, then connect them to the seedling experiment.

Mean and median answer slightly different questions

The mean adds all observed values and divides by their number. The median is the middle of the ordered values, or the mean of the middle two for an even number of values. Both describe a centre; neither replaces the observations.

growth_mm <- c(12, 14, 16)
mean(growth_mm)
median(growth_mm)

Both results are 14 mm. Now inspect a more uneven set:

uneven_mm <- c(10, 10, 10, 100)
mean(uneven_mm)
median(uneven_mm)

The mean is 32.5 mm and median 10 mm. Neither is an arithmetic error. The large observation affects the mean strongly. Investigate whether a large value is supported by the source record; do not delete it simply to make the mean and median agree. The appropriate description depends on the question and shape of the measurements.

Variance measures spread around the mean

For the three measurements 12, 14, and 16 mm, the deviations from their mean are −2, 0, and 2 mm. Their sum is zero, so an ordinary mean deviation would hide the spread. Squaring deviations prevents positive and negative values cancelling.

The sample variance adds squared deviations and divides by one less than the number of observations. The choice of this denominator helps estimate population variance from a sample; a derivation is beyond this course.

growth_mm <- c(12, 14, 16)
deviations_mm <- growth_mm - mean(growth_mm)
squared_deviations <- deviations_mm^2
sum(squared_deviations) / (length(growth_mm) - 1)
var(growth_mm)
sd(growth_mm)

The squared deviations are 4, 0, and 4. Their sum divided by 2 is 4 mm², the sample variance. The standard deviation, or SD, is its square root: 2 mm. SD is expressed in the original measurement’s units, which makes it easier to interpret alongside the mean. Variance uses squared units. Neither is the error in the mean or a claim that every plant lies within one SD.

close_mm <- c(13, 14, 15)
spread_mm <- c(8, 14, 20)
mean(close_mm)
mean(spread_mm)
sd(close_mm)
sd(spread_mm)

Both means are 14 mm. The SDs are 1 and 6 mm. The second set varies more among observations. A group with larger spread is not automatically measured less carefully: organisms can really differ. Measurement noise and biological variation can both contribute to observed spread.

Apply the descriptions to plants

Use the clean supplied table today so that variation is not confused with the repair exercise. Recreate all prerequisites in day12-variation.R.

library(readr)
library(dplyr)
seedlings <- read_csv("data/seedlings.csv", show_col_types = FALSE) |>
  mutate(growth_mm = final_mm - initial_mm)
control <- filter(seedlings, treatment == "control")
mean(control$growth_mm)
median(control$growth_mm)
var(control$growth_mm)
sd(control$growth_mm)

The control mean is about 15.3 mm and its SD about 3.19 mm. Report them with units and a sensible number of decimals; extra decimal places do not create more precise measurements. R may display more digits to help calculation, but those are not a requirement for prose.

Your practical

  1. Work through the three-measurement variance by hand, then compare with R.
  2. Predict the mean and SD if every value in close_mm increases by 10 mm. Check using mean(close_mm + 10) and sd(close_mm + 10).
  3. Predict what happens if lengths are converted from mm to cm by dividing by ten. Check the mean, variance, and SD in the new units.
  4. Calculate the treated group’s mean, median, variance, and SD. Write two sentences comparing centre and spread with the control group. The teacher may substitute the organic-laboratory transfer task below for this step; you do not need to complete both.
  5. Add a note explaining why “the average plant grew 15.3 mm” does not mean every control plant grew exactly that amount.

Transfer choice: variation in collected product

An organic synthesis is an experiment intended to make a product. In our simulated data/organic-recovery.csv, each row is one independent run. The supplied theoretical mass is the maximum product mass possible from that run’s starting materials; you do not need to derive it. Isolated mass is the dry product actually collected, assumed pure for this simplified calculation. Both are in grams. Calculate percentage yield as 100 × isolated mass / theoretical mass.

organic <- read_csv("data/organic-recovery.csv", show_col_types = FALSE) |>
  mutate(yield_percent = 100 * isolated_mass_g / theoretical_mass_g)
standard_runs <- filter(organic, protocol == "standard")
mean(standard_runs$yield_percent)
var(standard_runs$yield_percent)
sd(standard_runs$yield_percent)

Y01 gives 100 × 0.62 / 1.00 = 62%. For the six standard-protocol runs, the mean is about 68.67%, variance 18.67 squared percentage points, and SD 4.32 percentage points. A difference from 62% to 70% is eight percentage points, not an eight-percent relative increase. Repeat the summaries for the modified protocol and describe centre and spread in two sentences.

The runs used different starting amounts, so raw collected grams do not make the same comparison as percentage yield. A higher yield alone does not establish greater purity or a generally better method. Save this calculation in today’s script; no knowledge of reaction mechanisms is needed.

Hint: use the units to check yourself

Adding a constant shifts the centre but leaves deviations unchanged. Dividing lengths by ten divides the mean and SD by ten, but divides variance by one hundred. Check ?var and ?sd for the functions’ definitions and missing-value arguments.

Check your understanding

Which spread is larger?

The treated SD is about 4.87 mm, larger than the control SD. This describes variation among these plants; it does not by itself quantify uncertainty in the difference between group means. We will separate those questions next.

Extended practical — compare the effects of a shift and a scale change

Use about 75–90 minutes to make variation visible and explain it. Continue day12-variation.R. The purpose is to connect the arithmetic definition to units and actual observations, rather than learn another summary function. Choose your existing biological or organic context for the final write-up; complete the small numerical investigation below in either case.

  1. Make a three-row hand table for values 12, 14, and 16 mm. Include the mean, each signed deviation, squared deviation, their sum, and the denominator used for sample variance. Check the hand table against R. Save the table as a short text note or CSV, retaining the units in its explanation.
  2. Replace the last value with 26 mm. Predict the direction of the mean, median, variance, and SD changes before calculation. Then calculate them. Identify which statement about “a typical observation” is affected most by this one high value. A sensitivity demonstration does not authorise deleting an actual inconvenient measurement.
  3. Add 10 to every value in the original three-value vector. Explain why the signed deviations, variance, and SD stay the same while the mean changes. Draw both sets along the same horizontal scale, on paper or with a supplied point-plot pattern. An axis with inconsistent limits would obscure the shift.
  4. Convert the original vector from mm to cm by dividing by ten. Write the new mean, variance, and SD with units. Check that changing unit does not make the biological sample less variable. The numeric variance changes by a factor of 100 because its unit changes from mm² to cm².
  5. For your selected supplied context, compute centre and spread separately within both groups. For plants use growth_mm; for organic runs first use the supplied 100 * isolated_mass_g / theoretical_mass_g relationship to obtain yield_percent. Never compare raw grams across different starting scales and call that a comparison of yield.
  6. Make an individual-observation plot and add one mean marker per group using the familiar separate-summary-table pattern. Find at least one observation above and one below its group’s mean. Explain why a mean need not itself be an observed value and why showing only a mean conceals these differences.
  7. Write a short response to this claim: “The group with the larger SD must have the less reliable mean and the worse experimental technique.” Separate variation among measured units from uncertainty in an estimate and from possible biological or measurement sources of spread. The SD alone cannot identify the source or evaluate the entire design.
Hint: check the transformation, not a memorised rule

For the original vector, the sample variance is 4 mm² and SD 2 mm. In cm, variance is 0.04 cm² and SD 0.2 cm. Adding 10 mm changes only the centre. For the changed vector c(12, 14, 26), ask R for mean(), median(), var(), and sd() after writing predictions. Keep the two vectors under distinct names.

Independent transfer — describe another set without copying its labels

Allow about 60–90 minutes. Import data/seedlings-transfer.csv into an object called transfer and calculate final minus initial length. These are 18 new independent plants, nine per randomised group, with lengths in mm and complete measurements. The seven-day study uses the same column structure as the first experiment. Your job is to demonstrate that the reasoning transfers to new values.

  1. Start a separate script, day12-transfer.R, containing its own package loading, import, checks, and derived growth. Identify T01 and verify its subtraction by hand. A plausible overall mean would not reveal a sign error.
  2. Compute mean, median, sample variance, and SD for each treatment. Save a labelled table to output/day12-transfer-summaries.csv. State n alongside the summaries, even though neither group has missing values here.
  3. Draw both groups’ individual measurements on a common scale. Use your summaries to describe which group has the larger mean and which has the larger SD. Do not assume that the group with the larger mean must have the larger spread. Use actual values rather than the first experiment’s numbers.
  4. Convert the derived growth to cm in another column. Verify one group mean, SD, and variance in the new units. Preserve the mm column so the conversion is transparent. Give a sentence explaining which numeric changes reflect unit choice and which comparisons between groups remain the same.
  5. Write a 120–180 word explanation for a lab partner who sees only your table and figure. Define what SD describes, include units and independent sample sizes, and state one thing the descriptive summaries cannot establish. Avoid language saying that all treated plants are alike or that a larger mean implies every treated observation exceeds every control observation.
  6. Reopen the output table in another application and verify that it contains summaries, not the original plant-level rows. Restart R and regenerate the outputs. Keep the code, plot, and explanatory note together in your project.

Additional outputs: output/day12-transformations.txt, the transfer script, summary CSV, output/day12-transfer.png, and your interpretation. Checkable anchors: T01 grew 15.0 mm; both groups have nine observations. Save your own calculated group values rather than copying a worked answer before analysis.
